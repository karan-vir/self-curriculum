Self-curriculum experiment

Does a small model learn more by choosing its own training problems?

A fixed mix of only 8–10-digit sums, with no adaptation at all, did at least as well as the self-curriculum (87.1% against 84.7%). Both beat a fixed mix of all lengths (78.9%). Counting the budget in training examples, spending it on the hardest problems the model can still sometimes solve looks like what mattered; this run gives no evidence that adapting adds anything on top.

Caveats: Arm C's 8–10-digit range was chosen after seeing the results, and long sums give each example more answer tokens to learn from. Counted in generated tokens instead of examples, the self-curriculum comes out ahead (+3.8 points, an exploratory, after-the-fact comparison).

1Objective

Find out whether a small language model learns more, measured as accuracy on held-out problems, when it chooses its own training problems than when it follows a fixed curriculum, for the same number of training examples.

Self-improving models increasingly train on data they generate themselves, keeping only what a checker accepts. Which problems they practise on then decides how much each training example is worth. If a model can steer itself toward problems it can just barely solve, it should waste less compute on problems it already knows or can't yet attempt.

Question
Given 512 training examples per round, does training at the model's own edge (Arm A) reach higher held-out accuracy than a uniform mix of difficulties (Arm B)?
Follow-up
Added after the ten-seed results: does Arm A win by adapting, or simply by spending its budget on long sums? Arm C, a fixed mix of 8–10 digits, tests this.
Testbed
Adding two N-digit integers, N from 2 to 10, on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with LoRA. Difficulty can be set exactly and every answer checked exactly, so no labels or judge model are needed.
Measured
Learning efficiency: held-out accuracy per round, overall and per length, and the longest length the model solves at least half the time.
Not measured
General reasoning or transfer to other tasks. In this first version the model chooses the difficulty, not the problems themselves.
Success
Arm A sits above Arm B at matched rounds across seeds, or reaches longer sums. The plan was 3 seeds; the run grew to 10.

2Why this baseline matters

The experiment asks whether a small model learns more when it picks its own training problems at the edge of its ability than when it trains on a fixed mix, given the same number of training examples. Before either arm runs, we need to know where the edge is, how sharp it is, and what kind of mistakes the model makes, because Arm A trains only on the model's own correct answers at that edge. Section 3 sets out the design, section 4 the data and section 5 explains the edge.

The untrained model gets 44.0% of held-out additions right (792 of 1,800). Accuracy falls smoothly from 91.5% on 2-digit sums to 3.0% on 10-digit sums, which puts its starting edge at 5–6 digits. All 1,008 wrong answers parsed as numbers. The full evaluation takes 30 s on an M4 Pro. In the training precision the same model scores 43.9%, which is the round 0 figure used for every arm.

This tab covers the untrained model and the design. The trained results are in the Ten rounds tab.

3Experiment design

Hypothesis: for the same number of training examples, a model that trains at its own edge reaches higher held-out accuracy, or solves longer sums, than one trained on a fixed mix of difficulties.

Every arm starts from the same untrained model and runs the same loop for 10 rounds. Arms A and B differ only in who picks the difficulty. Arm A measures itself each round and trains where it's partly right. Arm B trains on a uniform mix of 2–10 digits every round, whatever the model can already do. Arm C does the same as Arm B, but only on 8–10 digits.

Arm A: self-curriculum

  1. Probe. Ask the current model 64 fresh problems at each length from 2 to 10 digits and measure greedy accuracy.
  2. Pick the edge. Keep the lengths with accuracy from 30% to 70%. If none qualify, take the one closest to 50%.
  3. Sample. Draw new problems at the edge lengths and let the model try each one 4 times at temperature 0.8.
  4. Keep only correct answers. The checker verifies each try exactly. Problems are drawn until 512 correct examples are collected.
  5. Train. One LoRA epoch on those 512 examples.
  6. Evaluate on the frozen 1,800-problem held-out set, then start the next round from the updated model.

Arm B: fixed curriculum (control)

Each round draws problems evenly across all nine lengths, with no probe and no edge. The model then tries each problem the same way as in Arm A, and the same 512-example budget, checker and LoRA settings apply. Arm B also trains only on the model's own correct answers, drawing more problems at a length until enough are solved.

Arm C: hard-only

Identical to Arm B, except that it draws evenly across 8, 9 and 10 digits only. There is no probe and no adaptation, and it trains only on the model's own correct answers. It was added after the ten-seed results, to test whether Arm A wins by adapting or simply by spending its budget on long sums. It may draw up to 200 batches of problems per length, against 50 for the other arms, so it can reach the budget while long sums are still rarely solved. It never needed more than 38, so the higher limit made no difference.

Arm B+key: reference

Identical to Arm B, except that a problem the model never solves is trained on the true answer. It isn't a control for Arm A, because it gets answers Arm A never sees. It shows how much the answer key adds on top of self-training.

Keeping the comparison fair

  • Same number of training examples. Every arm trains on exactly 512 examples per round with identical settings. That is not the same amount of supervision: the loss is on the answer tokens, and arms that train on longer sums get more of them. The mean operand length of the training examples is 8.6 digits for Arm A, 9.0 for Arm C and 6.0 for Arms B and B+key, or roughly 10.2, 10.6 and 7.6 answer tokens per example, end-of-turn included (estimated from the logged length mix).
  • Inference is reported, not matched. Arm A spends extra generation on probing. Every arm logs generated tokens so that cost is visible next to the result.
  • No test leakage. Any training problem that appears in the held-out set is rejected.
  • Ten seeds per arm. Small LoRA runs vary between seeds, so curves show the mean and range. The first three seeds looked promising but inconclusive, so seven more were added.
  • Arm C came after the results. Its 8–10-digit range was chosen knowing where Arm A's gain appeared, so it had an advantage Arm A didn't have.

How the result is read

  • Main plot: held-out accuracy against round for every arm, overall and per length.
  • Secondary: the longest length with at least 50% accuracy, per round.
  • Support for the hypothesis: Arm A's curve sits above Arm B's at matched rounds across seeds, or reaches longer sums.

What one problem looks like

Two integers with exactly N digits each (no leading zero), drawn uniformly at random, so carries occur as often as random digits produce them. The model sees the same prompt in every set:

Compute the sum. Reply with the number only.
23821 + 28970 =

wrapped in Qwen's chat template. A training example pairs that prompt with the model's own verified reply plus Qwen's end-of-turn marker, 52791<|im_end|>. The loss is computed on those answer tokens only, never on the prompt. Qwen reads numbers one digit per token, so each digit of the answer is its own prediction.

Keeping the sets apart

  • No test leakage. Probe and training problems that match a held-out problem are rejected and redrawn. Matching ignores operand order, so 12 + 34 and 34 + 12 count as the same problem.
  • One example per problem. Within a round, each training problem contributes at most one example (its first correct try), so the 512 examples are 512 different problems.
  • Repeats across rounds are possible but rare. Training rounds don't exclude each other. At 5 digits there are about 4 billion distinct problems, so a repeat is negligible. At 2 digits there are only 4,095, and the held-out set already uses 200 of them (4.9%), which matters for Arm B's uniform mix.
  • Training answers come from the model. Labels are the model's own replies, accepted only when the checker confirms them. Only the Arm B+key reference uses the true sum, when the model gets no try right, and it logs how often.

5What is the edge?

The edge is the range of lengths where the model is right some of the time: not most of the time, and not almost never. Here it means accuracy from 30% to 70%. For the untrained model that's 5 digits (58.5%) and 6 digits (39.5%).

Arm A learns only from its own answers that the checker confirms. That makes the useful zone narrow:

ZoneExampleWhat training there gives
Too easy2 digits · 91.5%Plenty of correct answers, but the model already knows these. Little to learn.
Edge5–6 digits · 39.5–58.5%Enough correct answers to train on, on problems the model doesn't yet solve reliably.
Too hard10 digits · 3.0%Almost no correct answers to keep. Most problems give nothing usable, even with 4 tries.

Accuracy of the untrained model on the 1,800-problem held-out set (200 per length), as in the results chart. During training, Arm A never uses the held-out set to pick its edge: it probes 64 fresh problems per length each round.

The edge is where a correct answer is both available and informative, much as a student learns most from problems they get right about half the time.

The edge moves. Arm A re-measures it every round. If training works, 5–6 digits become easy and the band shifts to 6–7, then 7–8. That moving target is the self-curriculum. Arm B ignores it.

Why the baseline looks at three things:

  • Where the edge is sets what Arm A trains on in round 1. With no length in the band, the loop would have nothing to start from.
  • How sharp it is means how fast accuracy falls with length. A sharp drop (95% at 4 digits, then 5% at 5) leaves the band empty and Arm A on its fallback, possibly swinging between rounds. Ours falls 13–19 points per digit, so two lengths sit cleanly inside.
  • What kind of mistakes it makes gives a before picture. Training on correct answers never shows the model its errors, so the error profile is how we tell what improved: whether phantom and dropped carries vanish at 5–6 digits, and whether units-column failures move to longer sums.

Greedy vs sampled. The baseline measures the edge with greedy decoding (one most-likely answer). Arm A collects training answers by sampling 4 tries at temperature 0.8. Each sampled try is usually a little less accurate, but the chance that at least one of 4 is right is higher, so the useful band in practice may sit slightly toward longer sums. That's one reason Arm A re-probes every round.

6Assumptions

  • Difficulty means operand length. Both operands have exactly N digits (no leading zeros), N from 2 to 10. Carries are not controlled; they occur as often as random digits produce them.
  • The prompt is fixed. “Compute the sum. Reply with the number only.” followed by a + b =, wrapped in Qwen's chat template, which also adds Qwen's default system message.
  • The first integer in the reply is the answer. Commas are stripped. Text after the first number is ignored, so a reply that states the right number and then rambles counts as correct.
  • Greedy decoding, 16 new tokens. Enough for an 11-digit sum. Greedy makes the run deterministic for a given machine.
  • Half precision on Apple's GPU. The model ran in float16 on MPS. CUDA in bfloat16 could shift individual answers, so treat differences of a point or two between machines as noise.
  • One test sample. 200 problems per difficulty is one draw. At 50% accuracy, that gives a 95% interval of about ±7 points.

7Methodology

  1. Freeze a held-out set. scripts/make_test_set.py draws 200 distinct problems per difficulty with seed 1234 (1,800 total) and writes them to data/test_set.jsonl. Problems are deduplicated regardless of operand order, and training later rejects any problem in this set.
  2. Generate. scripts/baseline.py runs the model on all 1,800 prompts in batches of 64 with left padding.
  3. Check exactly. Each reply is parsed to an integer and compared with a + b. No partial credit.
  4. Summarise. Accuracy per difficulty with 95% Wilson intervals, then the edge: difficulties with accuracy in [0.3, 0.7].
  5. Classify errors. Every wrong answer is sorted into one of three types and compared digit by digit, column by column, against the true sum.
One digit off
Same length as the true sum, and exactly one column is wrong (1294 vs 1194).
Several digits off
Same length, but two or more columns are wrong.
Wrong length
The reply has more or fewer digits than the true sum (109 for 99).
Phantom carry
Exactly one column is 1 too high, and no carry actually comes into that column.
Dropped carry
Exactly one column is 1 too low, and a carry should have come into that column.

8Untrained baseline: results

These are the untrained model's results, before any arm trains. Accuracy drops 13 to 19 points per extra digit from 3 to 7 digits, then flattens near zero from 8 digits up. Two difficulties fall inside the edge band.

9Untrained baseline: insights

  1. The edge is wide and well placed.

    Accuracy declines gradually instead of dropping off a cliff, so the 30–70% band picks out real difficulties (5 and 6 digits) and the empty-edge fallback is unlikely to trigger in round 1. Arm A has four harder levels above the edge to grow into.

  2. Digit count is a sound difficulty knob.

    Within a length, more carries don't lower accuracy (at 4 digits, if anything, the reverse), while accuracy falls steeply with length. Using operand length as the difficulty axis measures what actually makes the problem hard for this model.

  3. Short sums fail on carry bookkeeping.

    Below 6 digits, 60% of errors have a single wrong digit, most often by exactly 1 in a column where a carry was invented or lost. Phantom carries are the more common of the two: 46 against 31 below 6 digits, and 107 against 68 over all lengths. Training on its own correct answers should fix these first.

  4. Columns that sum to 9 trip the model up.

    Exploratory check, added after the main results. Call a column a sum-9 column when its two digits add to 9 and no carry comes into it, so its digit is 9 and it passes no carry on. On 2–6-digit problems, the untrained model gets 50.6% of problems with at least one such column right (122 of 241), against 76.4% of problems without one (580 of 759). It is lower at every one of those lengths, most at 5 digits (33% against 70%), and the gap holds within lengths (Mantel–Haenszel test stratified by length, p < 0.001). Phantom carries concentrate where the column to the right sums to 7–9, most at 9. Among the 89 wrong 2–6-digit answers that are too high by exactly a power of ten in a column other than the units, the column just to the right sums to 9 with no carry coming in for 37 (42%), to 8 for 21 (24%) and to 7 for 16 (18%), although only about 10%, 9% and 8% of digit pairs have those sums. The model seems to treat a column close to 10, and above all a 9, as if it already carries. A sum-9 column that does receive a carry, whose 0 passes the carry on, shows no consistent drop: accuracy is lower with one at 3 and 4 digits (81% against 88%, 64% against 76%) but not at 2, 5 or 6. Quirke & Barez (2023) singled out the same kind of column as the hard case in a small transformer trained on addition: whether a sum-9 column passes on a carry depends on the columns to its right. Their one-layer model failed when the carry did arrive and had to cascade (445 + 555); this model fails the other way, adding a carry that never arrives. Columns and carries are defined in scripts/sum9_check.py; results in results/sum9_check.json.

  5. Long sums fail on keeping columns straight.

    At 8+ digits even the units column, which needs no carry, goes wrong over a third of the time, and errors are spread across every column. That pattern points to the model losing track of which digits line up, not to faulty carry rules.

  6. The top of the range is at the floor.

    8 digits (8.5%) and 9 digits (10.0%) can't be told apart; their intervals overlap almost completely. Gains up there will show late and need more than 200 test problems to resolve.

11Trained results

Everything above describes the untrained model. The results of training, for all four arms over 10 rounds and 10 seeds, are in the Ten rounds tab, together with the compute comparison, what was planned and what was added later, the limitations and related work.

Ten rounds, four arms

A fixed mix of long sums, with no adaptation, does at least as well as the self-curriculum. Across ten seeds, Arm A beats the uniform mix, and Arm C does too. Arm C averages 87.1% after 10 rounds, against 84.7% for Arm A and 78.9% for Arm B. It is ahead of Arm B on all 10 seeds (8.2 points, p < 0.001). It is ahead of Arm A on 9 of 10 seeds, but that gap is borderline (2.4 points, p = 0.056) and nearly all from the last round.

Main findings
  • Self-curriculum beats the uniform mix by round 10: Arm A − Arm B +5.8 points (95% interval +1.7 to +10.0, p = 0.011, ahead on 9 of 10 seeds), and +5.9 over the last three rounds (p = 0.003). It trails at round 1 (−7.7 points, behind on 9 of 10) and is level at round 2. The seven seeds added after an interim look give +5.8 on their own (p = 0.055, ahead on 6 of 7). Planned arms; seed count, test and final-round readout chosen after an interim look at 3 seeds · moderate confidence
  • A fixed mix of 8–10-digit sums beats the uniform mix by more: Arm C − Arm B +8.2 points (+5.1 to +11.3, p < 0.001, 10 of 10 seeds). Added after the results · high confidence in the gap, its range chosen with hindsight
  • Adapting showed no accuracy gain at equal examples: Arm A − Arm C −2.4 points (−4.8 to +0.1, p = 0.056, A ahead on 1 of 10); level over rounds 2–9 and −0.3 over the last three rounds (p = 0.78). Inconclusive: unlike A − B, the gap does not hold over the last three rounds
  • At equal generated tokens, Arm A is ahead of Arm C, because Arm C spends 42% of its tokens in round 1: +3.8 points at Arm A's final token total (+0.4 to +7.1, p = 0.031, 8 of 10). Exploratory, after the fact · low confidence
  • Gains carry past the trained range: on 11–13 digits the round-10 models score 43–61% against 1.8% untrained; Arm A and Arm C can't be told apart there. Added after the results · high confidence against untrained

R1Accuracy by round

Every arm starts from the same 43.9% (round 0, evaluated in the training precision). After round 1, Arm C (74.3%) and Arm B (71.6%) are well ahead of Arm A (63.9%), which trains only at 5–7 digits in that round. Arm A then catches Arm C: from round 2 to round 9 the two are level (mean gap +0.2 points, p = 0.90). Arm C then gains 2.4 points in round 10 while Arm A stays at 84.7%. Arm B stays near 77–79% from round 6 on.

The planned secondary outcome can't separate the arms. The longest length with at least 50% held-out accuracy reaches 10 digits, the top of the range, by round 1–6 on every run (Arm A round 2–4, Arm B 1–4, B+key 1–6, Arm C 1–3) and is at 10 in 74 to 89 of each arm's 100 rounds.

In the summary table, "first round ≥ 80%" counts a run that never reaches 80% (Arm B seed 8, Arm B+key seed 1) as later than round 10 when taking the median. Round-by-round scores before round 10 use the 16-token cap, which was only re-checked for round 10 (R9).

R2Where Arm A chose to train

Each round Arm A probes 64 fresh problems per length and trains on lengths it gets 30–70% right. On every seed the edge started at 5–7 digits and first included 10 digits by round 2–4. After round 4 it stayed on 7–10 digits, apart from one round on seed 3 that also included 5 digits. From round 6 on, 33 of 50 rounds trained on a single length, nearly always 9 or 10 digits. In 15 of those 33 nothing fell inside the band, so it fell back to the length closest to 50%. The task stops at 10 digits, so the edge had nowhere further to go.

R3Final accuracy by length

Arm C finishes highest or level with the highest at every length: highest from 4 to 10 digits, including 4–7, which it never trained on, and within a point of the others at 2 and 3. Against Arm B its lead is clear from 6 to 10 digits (8.0 to 17.7 points, each p ≤ 0.005). Against Arm A, only the 8-digit gap (6.3 points, p < 0.001) would survive a correction for testing nine lengths, and over the last three rounds even that shrinks to 2.1 points on the means (79.1% against 77.0%). Arm A's lead over Arm B grows with length, from 5 points at 6 digits to 14.7 at 10 digits, and it is level with Arm B on 2–5 digits.

R4What each arm cost

Training is matched at 512 examples per round. Inference isn't: Arm A pays for its probe, and the arms without an answer key pay for rejected tries on long sums. Arm A generated slightly fewer tokens than Arm B. Arm C generated the most, 43% more than Arm A, and 42% of its tokens came in round 1. No arm fell short of the budget.

R5Compute Exploratory · after the fact

This comparison was not planned. It was defined after the results above were seen, uses only the existing logs, and its tests are not corrected for multiple comparisons. The runs were matched on training examples (512 per round), not on generated tokens, so this asks a different question of the same runs: how accurate is each arm for the generation it has spent so far?

Tokens here are every token the model generated to collect training data, counting Arm A's probe and rejected tries, and not counting held-out evaluation. Arm C's extra cost is front-loaded in round 1: it spent 213k tokens there (42% of its 509k total), while the untrained model rarely solved 8–10-digit sums. After round 1, per-round cost is similar for Arms A, B and C, at 36k, 30k and 33k tokens per round on average. Arm B+key spends about 18k per round throughout, because a problem it can't solve is trained on the true answer instead of being redrawn.

R6Beyond 10 digits

No arm ever trained past 10 digits, but all of them can do much longer sums than the untrained model. Scored on new 11–20-digit problems with no further training, the round-10 models average 43–61% on 11–13 digits, against 1.8% untrained, and Arm C still gets 16.8% at 17 digits. Arm C and Arm A both clearly beat Arm B on 11–13 digits, but the gap between Arm A and Arm C is not distinguishable from zero. The order is the one already seen at 10 digits, where accuracy drops only 5–9 points to 11 digits in every arm, so this shows the existing gaps carrying on, not one arm generalising better. Paired tests are uncorrected.

R7Insights

  1. A fixed long-sum mix matches or beats Arm A.

    Arm C beats Arm B by 8.2 points (95% interval 5.1 to 11.3, p < 0.001), on all 10 seeds, with no probe and no adaptation. That is more than Arm A's 5.8-point lead over Arm B, so this run gives no evidence that adapting adds accuracy beyond choosing long sums. Arm A also ends up in nearly the same place: it trained only on 8–10 digits in 67 of 100 rounds, but only once across all seeds in rounds 1–2.

  2. Adapting showed no accuracy gain over Arm C.

    Arm C finishes 2.4 points ahead of Arm A (95% interval −0.1 to 4.8, p = 0.056), and ahead on 9 of 10 seeds. This doesn't show Arm C is truly better, because the gap rests on one round. It shrinks to 0.3 over the last three rounds (p = 0.78), and the two are level across rounds 2–9. Seed 3, where Arm A fell to 77.7%, contributes much of the size of the gap: without it the gap is 1.4 points, smaller but more consistent (p = 0.022, Arm C ahead on 8 of 9). That last-three-rounds check is why this gap is read as inconclusive while Arm A's lead over Arm B, which holds over the last three rounds (+5.9 points, p = 0.003), is read as moderate. What is clear is that Arm A doesn't finish above a fixed curriculum on long sums, which adapting would need to do to justify itself on accuracy.

  3. Adapting may still have value this run can't show.

    Someone had to choose 8–10 digits for Arm C, and that choice was made after seeing where Arm A gained. Arm A found those lengths with no prior knowledge. This run can't measure what that is worth, because a good fixed range exists here and is easy to guess. Arm D in R11, which adapts only in round 1, would test part of this.

  4. Training only on long sums did not cost accuracy on shorter ones.

    Arm C never trains below 8 digits, yet it finishes above Arm B at 4–7 digits, by 3.7, 5.5, 8.0 and 8.8 points (each p < 0.05); at 2 and 3 digits the two are level. (Grouping 2–7 digits, 4.5 points, p = 0.005, is a grouping chosen after the fact.) Practising every length gave Arm B no visible edge on the short ones. These results don't show why. One possibility is that long sums contain the same column steps as short ones, many times over.

  5. Arm A's slow start is not explained by focusing on few lengths.

    Arm B leads Arm A after round 1 on 9 of 10 seeds, by 7.7 points, and Arm A's round-1 score varies widely across seeds (55.9% to 71.6%). An earlier version of this report put the gap down to Arm B practising every length at once. Arm C argues against that: it practises three lengths and leads Arm A by 10.4 points after round 1, on all 10 seeds, while roughly matching Arm B (+2.7 points, ahead on 5 of 10). One possible cause, not tested here, is Arm A's first edge of 5–7 digits; Arm D in R11 would test it. Arm A does recover quickly, reaching 80% at a median of round 4, against 4.5 for Arm C and 7 for Arm B.

  6. Arm A still beats the uniform mix, and on long sums.

    Arm A finishes ahead of Arm B on 9 of 10 seeds, by 5.8 points (95% interval 1.7 to 10.0, p = 0.011). Averaging the last three rounds gives 5.9 points (p = 0.003), and leaving out seed 6, where Arm B had an unusually bad last round, gives 4.5 (p = 0.010). The seven seeds added after the first three give a similar gap on their own (5.8 points, p = 0.055), though that subset alone is not conclusive.

  7. One seed can say almost anything.

    The gap between Arm A and Arm B ranges from −3.6 points on seed 3 to +18.2 on seed 6. The gap between Arm A and Arm C ranges from −11.2 on seed 3 to +0.8 on seed 6. Single-seed comparisons of small LoRA runs are not reliable.

  8. The answer key shows no clear benefit.

    Arm B+key averaged 81.4% against Arm B's 78.9%, despite 880–1,221 answer-key examples per run. That gap of about 2.5 points is not distinguishable from zero (p = 0.21), and without seed 6 it is 0.9 points. Ten seeds can't rule out a small benefit. Choosing lengths mattered more: Arm C, with no answer key, beats B+key on all 10 seeds by 5.8 points (p = 0.003).

  9. Arm A may be steadier than Arm C, but it isn't clear.

    The worst one-round drop averaged 2.2 points per seed for Arm A, 3.8 for Arm C, 5.5 for B+key and 5.8 for Arm B. Arm A's drop was smaller than Arm B's on 9 of 10 seeds (p = 0.005), but the difference from Arm C is not distinguishable from zero (p = 0.19). Every arm still swings from round to round, so any single round's score is noisy.

  10. Arm C's extra cost is almost all in round 1.

    Arm C generated about 509k tokens per run, against 356k for Arm A, 371k for Arm B and 176k for B+key. Of Arm C's total, 213k came in round 1, when the untrained model solved only 15%, 12% and 8% of the 8-, 9- and 10-digit problems it drew (at least one of 4 sampled tries right; greedy held-out accuracy was about 8–10% at 8–9 digits and 3% at 10). After that it used about 33k per round, close to Arm A. Runs took about 28 minutes, against 24 for Arm A. Arm C's 512 examples were reached every round, so the extra draws cost time but left no shortfall.

  11. The difficulty range is now the limit.

    Arm A's edge reached 10 digits by round 2–4 on every seed, and in 16 of 100 rounds every length probed above 70%. Every arm is limited by the 10-digit ceiling, which also makes Arm C's range easy to guess.

  12. Interrupted runs.

    Three Arm A runs were resumed from a saved round after a power problem or hang (seed 0 after round 9, seed 4 after round 1, seed 7 after round 3), and one Arm B run (seed 2) was rerun from the start. Their sampling sequence changes on resume, but every round already starts a fresh optimizer, so no training state is lost. Their results sit inside the normal range. All ten Arm C runs finished without a restart.

R9Limitations

  • One model. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct only. A larger or differently pretrained model could have a different edge, and the ranking of arms could change.
  • One task. Addition of two equal-length integers. Long sums contain the same column steps as short ones, so practice on one length transfers to others unusually well; on a task where skills transfer less, choosing what to practise may matter more.
  • LoRA only. Rank 16, one epoch per round, a fresh optimizer each round. Full fine-tuning or other settings were not tried.
  • Precision and hardware. The untrained baseline ran in float16 on Apple's MPS; training and every round's evaluation ran in float32 on MPS. Greedy replies can differ on other hardware or precisions, so differences of a point or so between machines are noise.
  • One held-out test set. 200 problems per length, drawn once. Every round and arm is scored on the same 1,800 problems, so its sampling error is shared and isn't in the seed-to-seed intervals.
  • The 10-digit ceiling. Arm A's edge reached 10 digits by round 2–4 on every seed and then had nowhere to go, so from then on it behaved much like a fixed hard-only mix.
  • Arm C had hindsight and a higher draw limit. Its 8–10-digit range was chosen after seeing where Arm A gained, and it could draw up to 200 batches per length against 50. It never needed more than 38, but the range choice is an advantage Arm A didn't have.
  • Equal examples, not equal supervision. Every arm trains on 512 examples per round, but the loss is on the answer tokens, and Arms A and C train on longer sums (mean operand length 8.6 and 9.0 digits, against 6.0 for B and B+key). An Arm C example carries roughly 1.4 times as many supervised tokens as an Arm B example, which may explain part of its lead.
  • Overall accuracy weights the nine lengths equally. At 2–4 digits every arm finishes near the ceiling (89–98%), so the differences in overall accuracy come almost entirely from 5–10 digits, mainly 6–10, which is where Arms A and C train. A different weighting of lengths would change the size of the gaps.
  • Uncorrected multiple comparisons. Many paired tests are reported across arms, lengths, rounds and subsets, none corrected. Single results near p = 0.05 should be read with that in mind, including the A − C gaps.
  • The 16-token cap. Held-out replies were cut at 16 new tokens, enough for an 11-digit answer. A check added after the results re-ran every round-10 model with 32 tokens: of 72,000 replies (four arms × ten seeds × 1,800), 271 reached 16 tokens without ending (Arm A 1, Arm B 49, Arm B+key 221, Arm C 0), almost all from two runs (Arm B seed 5 and Arm B+key seed 4) that added text after the answer, such as "Sum: Sum: …" or a "Step-by-Step Calculation" heading. In none of the 271 was any part of the number cut off, and no reply's score changes with 32 tokens; the first 16 tokens reproduce every logged round-10 score (360 of 360 arm, seed and length combinations). Script: scripts/check_token_cap.py; results in results/token_cap_check.json. Sums longer than 11 digits (R6) were scored with 32 tokens. Only round 10 was re-checked; earlier rounds' scores, used in R1 and R2, were not.
  • Interrupted runs. Three Arm A runs were resumed and one Arm B run restarted (R8). Their results sit inside the range of the other seeds, but their sampling sequences differ from an uninterrupted run.
  • Ten seeds. Enough to show the larger gaps, not to rule out small ones. A gap that is not distinguishable from zero is not evidence that two arms are equal.

R10Related work

  • STaR (Zelikman, Wu, Mu & Goodman, 2022, STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning, NeurIPS). Fine-tunes a model on its own rationales that reach the correct answer, and repeats; for problems it gets wrong it also "rationalizes", generating a rationale with the correct answer given as a hint. Arms A, B and C are STaR without rationalization and without rationales (the reply is only the number); Arm B+key, which trains on the true answer when no try is right, is the closest to rationalization. STaR restarts fine-tuning from the original model at each iteration; here each round continues from the previous round's model.
  • ReSTEM (Singh et al., 2023, Beyond Human Data: Scaling Self-Training for Problem-Solving with Language Models, TMLR). Generate, filter with a binary reward, fine-tune, repeat, at much larger scale on maths and code. The same self-training loop; this study adds a choice of difficulty on top.
  • Curriculum learning (Bengio, Louradour, Collobert & Weston, 2009, Curriculum Learning, ICML). Training from easy to hard can speed learning. Arms B and C are fixed curricula with no ordering; a fixed easy-to-hard ramp is the stronger control proposed in R11.
  • Teacher-Student Curriculum Learning (Matiisen, Oliver, Cohen & Schulman, 2017). A teacher picks subtasks by learning progress, the slope of the student's learning curve on each, and was tested on decimal addition with LSTMs among other tasks. Arm A uses a different rule: a fixed competence band, training where probe accuracy is between 30% and 70%, with no measure of progress.
  • Absolute Zero (Zhao et al., 2025, Absolute Zero: Reinforced Self-play Reasoning with Zero Data). A model proposes its own tasks and solves them, with a code executor checking both. The proposer is rewarded with one minus the solver's average success rate on a task, and with zero when the solver never or always solves it (their Eq. 4), so it favours tasks that are rarely but sometimes solved. Arm A's 30–70% band is a cruder preference for partly solved problems, and the model here picks only the difficulty, not the problems.
  • Teaching Arithmetic to Small Transformers (Lee, Sreenivasan, Lee, Lee & Papailiopoulos, 2023; ICLR 2024). Shows that data format and intermediate steps strongly affect how small transformers learn addition, and that length generalisation is hard. Here the format is fixed and the model is pretrained; only the choice of training lengths varies.
  • Understanding Addition in Transformers (Quirke & Barez, 2023; ICLR 2024). Reverse-engineers a one-layer transformer trained on addition and finds that columns summing to 9, whose carry depends on the columns to their right, are the hard case. Section 9 finds sum-9 columns are also a hard case for the untrained Qwen model, though it errs in the opposite direction.

R11Next steps

  • Arm D, edge then hard. Round 1 as in Arm A (probe, train at the edge), rounds 2–10 as in Arm C (fixed 8–10 digits). Arm A's only clear weakness is round 1, and Arm C's only extra cost is round 1. Arm D tests whether adapting matters only for choosing where to start: if it matches Arm C on accuracy at Arm A's token cost, that is what adapting bought here.
  • A second task where skills transfer less, for example multiplication, where practising long problems may not cover short ones. That is where choosing what to practise should matter most.
  • More rounds, to see whether Arm C's final-round lead over Arm A lasts or was noise, and how the token comparison in R5 changes once round 1 is a smaller share of the total.
  • A fixed ramp curriculum that moves from short to long sums on a schedule set in advance. It is a stronger control than a uniform mix, and separates "adapting" from simply "getting harder over time".
  • Not the lead item: extending to 2–20 digits. An earlier version of this report put this first. Two things argue against it. The models already extrapolate past 10 digits without training there (R6), so a longer range would partly measure that. And a hard-only arm set beyond the model's reach would stall trivially, because it would find almost no correct answers to train on; beating it would show little about adapting.
